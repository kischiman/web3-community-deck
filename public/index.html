01 · Proposal

Digital technologies for community-building and social resilience

Three neighbourhoods in one planning district, matched on socio-demographic profile and built environment. Baseline survey: 100 people each. Common theme — a caring community.

Goals

  • Understand how ground-up community-building initiatives that build social resilience can be facilitated through digital apps.
  • Answer the core question: which of the two generations — Web 2.0 or Web 3.0 — is more effective at letting citizens address a common issue together, and which features make the difference.
  • Define effectiveness twice over: how quickly and sustainably a project is realised, and how strongly trust, social capital and social beliefs improve.
  • Produce a report and journal publications, a community engagement manual, and a policy brief for MCCY, MCI and IMDA.

Hypotheses

H1
Social resilience is strengthened when people work together, ground-up, to identify and address common needs — with spillover onto the wider neighbourhood. Without physical contact, collaboration rarely extends past a small group of trusted initiators.
H2
Web 2.0 apps in addition to physical contact are more effective than physical contact alone.
H3
Web 3.0 creates conditions for deeper connection — permissionless membership, co-creation, norm-setting, verified pro-social behaviour — and so enhances resilience and delivery more than any other form of digital engagement.

Argentina Onchain Residency

San Martín de los Andes · 1–15 November 2025 · two weeks in person · part of Edge Patagonia, ahead of Devconnect Buenos Aires · 50+ builders, researchers and entrepreneurs with Argentine stakeholders from government, business, universities, cooperatives and civil society

Read the full paper on SSRN

How it ran

A six-step method — Participatory Unblocking of Blockchain Use Cases — built on a methodological inversion: start from concrete problems and the barriers to adoption, then ask whether an onchain system is feasible, appropriate and desirable.

  1. Choose the place and define the problem scope
  2. Structured discovery of local needs that could genuinely benefit from blockchain's guarantees
  3. Recruit local and international builders against the selected problem statements
  4. Convene in person — talks and workshops on economic, socio-political and cultural context, and on what has already been tried
  5. A time-bounded design sprint, with continuous feedback from the people closest to each problem
  6. Connect local users to builders after the event, to support sustained implementation

What came out

21 problem statements from 10+ organisations — public procurement, health logistics, elections and civic participation, municipal budgeting and open data, fintech rails, SME treasury, ticketing and artist royalties, industrial certification, NGO operations, university credentialing.

Seven working prototypes in the second week: Teach ID (verifiable teacher credentials), Córdobras (onchain procurement records and asset passports), Señalica (liquid democracy with privacy-preserving voting), VotAR (WhatsApp-based voting for low-cost organisational elections), MatePlus (community loyalty keeping value local), COTEPAY (community-owned payments channelling float into yield for members), Constellation (place-based coordination using escrow and portable reputation).

What was learned

  • Contextualised, participatory design measurably narrows the gap between promise and utility — prototypes should be grounded in documented coordination failures, not speculative use cases.
  • Embedding inside a larger event created attentional trade-offs; two weeks gave immersion but at a relaxed pace; remoteness raised coordination costs and limited institutional participation.
  • Builders were not paid to build specific solutions, so they followed their own interests — collaborative in spirit, but some problem statements went unaddressed.
  • Next time: narrower thematic scope, smaller curated cohorts, an explicit handoff plan per prototype, and incentives that ask problem-owners to commit resources upfront.

The Home Internet Project, Central Australia

Ellie Rennie et al. · a four-year project · three remote Aboriginal outstations — Kwale Kwale, Mungalawurru, Imangara · 20 computers into 20 homes · ~50 participants · with the Centre for Appropriate Technology, the Central Land Council, a consumer peak body and university researchers

Read the book chapter

How it ran

The intervention was a computer and a satellite internet connection installed in each of twenty homes across three outstations, kept running with training and maintenance for the length of the study, in three phases:

  1. Consultation. Initial contact through local organisations; elders in the three outstations gave permission, including formal ethics approval. Prior ICT use and perceived barriers documented before anything was installed.
  2. Living with it. Computers installed by August 2011. Structured interviews 3–4 times a year, one-on-one and in groups. Visits of a day or two per community every six weeks, much of it spent in houses responding to on-the-spot requests. Two years of training and maintenance support produced 1,458 event records. Usage data extracted mid-project with owners' permission to check that stated use matched actual use. Comparison sites: Papunya (shared facility) and Ali Curung (mobile coverage).
  3. Handing over. From the end of 2013, residents were helped to set up their own services and the transition to household-controlled accounts was documented, with visits continuing through 2014.

What was found

  • Seventeen of twenty computers were still operational after a year; only two were lost across the whole project.
  • The "communal lifestyle" assumption was wrong. People wanted to be identified as owners — padlocks, passwords, taking the cords away when travelling. Shared facilities were captured by particular families.
  • Putting computers in homes shifted the gender pattern: women were the most frequent user in two-thirds of households, against a telecentre elsewhere that became so identified with young men a separate women's room was needed.
  • Mobility — between houses and out of community — has consequences for ownership, billing and fixed infrastructure like satellite dishes.
  • The real barrier was the retail structure, not the desire. Post-paid billing, the only option, was intolerable to many; about half the owners struggled to hold a sufficient bank balance over the first six months. In Ali Curung, everyone paying for internet paid for pre-paid mobile broadband, though satellite was cheaper.

Long-term outcomes

  • The project produced tangible recommendations on infrastructure, on how to help households acquire services, and on the retail arrangements most likely to succeed.
  • It led to permanent infrastructure. The Centre for Appropriate Technology — a research partner — designed and tested a new kind of mobile hotspot towards the end of the project, and now installs them in outstations, at highway stops and other key locations. Non-electrical and low-maintenance, they use a satellite dish to amplify mobile reception from up to 40km outside mobile range.
  • Public Wi-Fi gained traction with policymakers on the back of this work: in 2016 the Northern Territory Government announced 34 community Wi-Fi projects, administered by Northern Territory Libraries.
  • The reframing is what made policy possible — treating the divide as a matter of digital choice rather than social exclusion. Households weren't behind; the offer on the table failed to meet their terms, so they went without rather than accept a bad arrangement.

Kaeru-chan, Japan

An IRB-compliant data collection pipeline for a field study — NFC-tagged objects that travel between participants, a phone-first journey, and a consent model built for a research ethics board rather than retrofitted to one.

Browse the source

How it works

Each object carries an NFC chip. Scanning it opens a web journey on the participant's own phone — no app to install, no account to create.

  1. Landing. The scan resolves to a per-chip page, framing the condition the participant has been assigned to.
  2. Consent. An explicit notice with Consent / No thanks, then a short demographics step. Declining ends the journey cleanly with a brief exit questionnaire.
  3. The journey. Participants log what they actually did — moved it, shared it, handed it to someone else — and can leave a postcard of text and a photo.
  4. Exit. A 1–7 scale survey, then a thank-you screen carrying a Withdraw all my data button.

Chip capacity turned out to be load-bearing: NTAG213 silently truncates mid-URL, so the study runs on NTAG215/216.

What makes it IRB-compliant

  • Withdrawal actually deletes. One transaction removes demographics, survey, scan and action rows together — not a flag, not a soft delete.
  • Photos are stripped of EXIF client-side, so location metadata never reaches the server in the first place.
  • Identity is separated from data. Participants appear as a UUID, and the table linking them to anything identifying is destroyed on a schedule — with the destruction itself logged.
  • Consent has a shelf life. Scheduled jobs abandon never-decided consents, time out inactive quests at 60 days, and send reminders at 30.
  • The audit trail is the interface. Every scheduled run — backups, linking destruction, abandonment, timeouts — is visible on the dashboard with its outcome, so compliance can be inspected rather than asserted.

03 · What works

Practices

Emergence

  • Observe. Ethnographic research before design — spend time with the people and the place, and document what is actually happening before proposing anything.
  • Design for emergent behaviours — set conditions and constraints, not outcomes. The system's job is to make good behaviour easy, not to prescribe it.

Trust and education

  • Teach how it works before asking anyone to use it. Comprehension is what turns interest into participation.
  • Earn trust. Make people trust you — adoption follows trusted recommendations.

Documentation

  • Test with users and research the market — build against a real, observed user and a clear picture of the external environment.
  • Document the internal structure — decision rights written down, and a shared understanding of what the team needs to function.
